Decision rights are the agreed answer to a simple question: for each important kind of decision, who makes it, who must be consulted, and who needs to know afterwards. When they are clear, decisions happen at the right level and at the right speed. When they are not, decisions drift upward until they all land on the founder's desk.

Most growing companies never set decision rights on purpose. They inherit them from the early days, when the founder decided everything and everyone was in the same room. That works for twenty people. It breaks somewhere past fifty, and by a few hundred it is usually the main reason execution feels slow.

## Three common frameworks

Several frameworks exist to make decision rights explicit. Three are widely used. They overlap, and none is universally better; the right one depends on what is going wrong.

### RACI

RACI assigns four roles to each task or decision:

- **Responsible:** does the work.
- **Accountable:** owns the outcome and signs it off. There should be exactly one.
- **Consulted:** gives input before the decision.
- **Informed:** is told afterwards.

RACI is the most familiar of the three and is good for clarifying who owns which piece of recurring work. Its weakness is that it was built for tasks more than decisions. Teams often end up with long lists of people marked "Consulted", which slows things down rather than speeding them up.

### RAPID

RAPID, developed at Bain & Company, is designed specifically for decisions. The letters are not in sequence order:

- **Recommend:** gathers input and proposes a course of action.
- **Agree:** must sign off on the recommendation, usually for legal, regulatory, or policy reasons. Use sparingly.
- **Perform:** carries out the decision.
- **Input:** provides facts and views. Their input is considered but not binding.
- **Decide:** makes the final call and commits the organisation to it. One person.

RAPID's strength is that it separates giving input from having a veto. Many slow decisions are slow because everyone with an opinion behaves as if they hold an "Agree". Naming the one "Decide" and keeping the "Agree" list short does most of the work.

### DACI

DACI, popularised by product teams, is a lighter variant:

- **Driver:** runs the decision process and keeps it moving.
- **Approver:** makes the decision. One person.
- **Contributors:** provide expertise and input.
- **Informed:** are told the outcome.

DACI is quick to adopt and works well for project and product decisions. It says less about recurring operational decisions, where RACI or a decision rights matrix is usually clearer.

## Which one to use

- **Nobody is sure who owns recurring work:** start with RACI.
- **Important decisions stall, or get reopened after they are made:** start with RAPID.
- **Projects drift because no one is driving the decision:** start with DACI.

Many companies mix them: a RACI for operational ownership, RAPID for the handful of high-stakes decisions that matter most. The framework matters less than writing the answer down and using it.

## The founder problem

In founder-led companies the real issue is rarely the framework. It is that the founder is the default "Decide" on almost everything, often without anyone choosing that. Common signs:

- Approvals queue up whenever the founder travels.
- Leaders bring decisions they could make themselves, because past decisions were overturned.
- The same decision is made twice: once by the team, then again by the founder.

Handing off decisions without losing control usually follows a pattern:

1. **List the decisions, not the tasks.** Pricing exceptions, hiring above a level, vendor contracts, budget moves. Twenty to forty decision types cover most of what matters.
2. **Set thresholds.** Decide by value, risk, or reversibility. A leader decides below a threshold; above it, the founder does. This is the core of a [delegation of authority](/glossary/delegation-of-authority).
3. **Name one decider per decision type.** Input can be broad. The decision cannot.
4. **Keep control explicit.** Controls built into the process (thresholds, reviews, audit trails) give the founder more control than informal approvals ever did, not less.
5. **Hold the line.** The fastest way to undo a decision rights framework is to overturn a decision made within it. If a threshold is wrong, change the threshold.
