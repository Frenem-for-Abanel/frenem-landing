Organisational network analysis (ONA) is a way of mapping how work really moves through a company: who people go to for information, decisions, help, and energy, regardless of what the org chart says. The output is a network. People are the points, and their working relationships are the lines between them.

An org chart shows who reports to whom. A network map shows who actually depends on whom. The two rarely match, and the gap between them is where most organisational surprises come from: the resignation nobody saw coming, the team that stalls when one person goes on leave, the two departments that should talk every week and never do.

## What ONA measures

Most ONA work comes back to a handful of patterns. The names vary between practitioners, but the ideas are stable.

- **Hidden brokers.** People who connect groups that would otherwise not talk. They rarely have the title to match. If they leave, two parts of the business quietly stop coordinating.
- **Overloaded hubs.** People everyone routes through. They look indispensable and usually are, which is the problem: they become a bottleneck and a burnout risk at the same time.
- **Isolation.** People, often new joiners or remote staff, with few working ties. Isolation tends to show up before disengagement and exit, so it is an early signal worth catching.
- **Silos and friction.** Teams with dense connections inside and thin ones outside. Some separation is healthy; too much means handoffs fail and decisions take the long way round.
- **The manager effect.** How a team's network changes under different managers. Two teams with the same structure can work very differently depending on who leads them.

In network terms these map to measures such as degree (how many ties someone has), betweenness centrality (how often someone sits on the shortest path between others), and density (how connected a group is internally). You do not need the maths to use the results, but it helps to know that the patterns are measured, not guessed.

## Survey-based and passive ONA

There are two broad ways to collect the data, and the choice shapes everything that follows.

### Passive ONA

Passive ONA reads metadata from the tools people already use: who emails whom, who meets whom, who messages whom in chat. It needs no effort from employees and can run continuously.

The trade-offs are real. It measures traffic, not trust: a weekly status email and a relationship someone relies on look the same. It misses work that happens in person or on the phone. And it raises a serious privacy question, because employees did not choose to take part. Even when only metadata is used, many people experience it as monitoring, and that perception can damage the very relationships being measured.

### Survey-based ONA

Survey-based (or active) ONA asks people directly, with short questions about who they work with and how: who they go to for information, who they rely on to get things done, where collaboration is hard. Every data point is an answer someone chose to give.

The trade-offs run the other way. It takes a few minutes of each person's time, it is a snapshot rather than a stream, and it depends on enough people answering honestly. In return it captures what metadata cannot: the quality of a relationship, not just its volume. Because people opt in, it is far easier to run in a way staff trust.

### Which one fits

Passive ONA suits large organisations with strong data governance that mainly want to understand communication load. Survey-based ONA suits leaders who want to understand relationships, risk, and friction, and who need employees to trust the process. For most founder-led and scaling companies, trust is the deciding factor: a diagnostic that people feel watched by tends to change the behaviour it is trying to measure.

## Privacy is a design choice, not a policy

Whichever method you use, how the results are protected matters as much as what they show. Good practice includes:

- **Response thresholds.** Team-level views only appear when enough people have taken part, so no one can be singled out by elimination.
- **Separate audiences.** Individuals see their own results; leadership sees patterns at team level and above, never named individual answers.
- **No content.** Never read the content of messages or documents. Relationship patterns are enough.
- **Plain explanations.** Tell people before they take part what is collected, who sees what, and why.

## What leaders do with the results

A network map is only useful if it changes decisions. Typical actions include:

1. **Protecting brokers.** Recognise the people holding groups together, spread their load, and plan for their absence before it happens.
2. **Fixing handoffs.** Where friction clusters between two teams, change the interface: shared rituals, clearer ownership of the handoff, or a different reporting line.
3. **Onboarding on purpose.** Connect isolated people early instead of hoping relationships form on their own.
4. **Redesigning around reality.** When the chart and the network disagree, ask which one is right. Sometimes the structure should change to match how work actually flows.
