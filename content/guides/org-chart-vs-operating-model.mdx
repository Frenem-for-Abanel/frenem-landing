An org chart and an operating model are often treated as the same thing. They are not. An org chart is a picture of reporting lines. An operating model is the full description of how the business turns strategy into results: who decides, who owns what, how work moves between teams, how performance is measured, and how the whole thing is governed. The org chart is one view of the operating model, and usually the least informative one.

## What an org chart tells you

An org chart answers a narrow set of questions well:

- Who reports to whom.
- How teams are grouped.
- How many people sit in each part of the business, and the [span of control](/glossary/span-of-control) of each manager.

That is genuinely useful. It is also where most companies stop, which is why the org chart gets asked to answer questions it cannot: who owns this outcome, who can approve that spend, why does this handoff keep failing.

## What an operating model adds

An [operating model](/glossary/operating-model) covers the parts of the organisation a chart cannot show:

- **Decision rights.** Who decides what, and at what threshold. See [decision rights frameworks](/guides/decision-rights-frameworks-rapid-raci-daci).
- **Ownership.** One owner for every outcome, written down, including the outcomes that sit between teams.
- **Roles and levels.** What each role is accountable for, how roles relate, and how people grow. This is [job architecture](/glossary/job-architecture).
- **Measurement.** What each role and team is measured on: [KRAs and KPIs](/glossary/kra-vs-kpi), goals, and review cycles.
- **Ways of working.** How work and information move between teams, including the informal network that never appears on the chart.
- **Governance.** How the business controls itself: delegation limits, reviews, audit trails, and channels for raising concerns.

## Why the difference matters

Most organisational problems live in the parts the org chart leaves out. A company can have a tidy chart and still be slow, because decisions route through a few people, ownership is ambiguous, and nobody is sure who is measured on what. Redrawing the chart does not fix any of that. Redesigning the operating model does.

The reverse is also true. A good operating model with an out-of-date chart creates confusion of its own: new joiners cannot find the right person, and leaders argue about ownership that was settled months ago.

## The problem of keeping it current

Both the chart and the operating model start going stale the day they are written down. People join and leave, teams reorganise, responsibilities shift. In many companies the "current" org chart is a slide someone updates once a quarter, and the operating model lives in a deck from the last restructure.

Keeping them current needs three things:

1. **A single record.** One place where reporting lines, ownership, measures, and governance live together, rather than a chart in one file, KPIs in a spreadsheet, and decision rights in a deck.
2. **Updates as part of the work.** Changes should be made when they happen, by the people who own them, not saved up for an annual clean-up.
3. **A history.** An audit trail of what changed and when, so governance and compliance questions have answers.
